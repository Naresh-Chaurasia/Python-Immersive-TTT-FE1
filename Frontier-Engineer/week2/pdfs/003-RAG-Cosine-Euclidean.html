Cosine similarity vs Euclidean distance

Two ways to ask "how similar are these vectors?" — and why they give different answers. Work through this page top to bottom: read the concept, use the interactive diagram, run the guided experiments, then check your understanding with the worked example.

Section 1 of 6

Building intuition first

Before any mathematics, fix these two mental images. Every formula you encounter later is just a precise way of measuring one of these two things.

∠

Cosine similarity — the compass

Two hikers set off from the same campsite. One walks north-northeast, the other walks north. They are heading in nearly the same direction — even if one walks 2 km and the other walks 10 km. Cosine similarity measures direction agreement, ignoring how far each hiker travels.

↔

Euclidean distance — the ruler

After an hour, you stretch a ruler between the two hikers' current positions. Euclidean distance measures that gap. If one hiker walked much further, the gap is large — even if they were heading in the same general direction.

🛒

The supermarket map

Imagine a map of a supermarket where products are placed by similarity: milk and yoghurt are close, apples and pears are nearby, motor oil is far away. Cosine similarity tells you if two items face the same "aisle direction". Euclidean distance tells you the shelf-gap between them.

💬

Why this matters for text

"My package is missing" and "Where is my order?" point in the same semantic direction. Their embeddings will have a small angle between them — high cosine similarity — even if the vectors have different lengths because the sentences differ in word count.

Section 2 of 6

The two concepts, side by side

Read each box carefully. Pay attention to what each formula ignores — that is the key to understanding when to use which metric.

Cosine similarity

Measures the angle between two vectors

Given vectors A and B, cosine similarity is the cosine of the angle θ between them.

cos(θ) = (A · B) / (|A| × |B|)

A · B = A1B1 + A2B2 + ... + AnBn
|A| = √(A1² + A2² + ... + An²)
  • Result range: −1 to +1 (embeddings: 0 to 1)
  • 1.0 = identical direction (fully similar)
  • 0.0 = perpendicular (completely unrelated)
  • Ignores magnitude — only direction counts
  • Preferred for NLP and text embeddings
Euclidean distance

Measures the straight-line gap between two points

The length of the line segment connecting the tips of vectors A and B in the same vector space.

d(A,B) = √(∑(Ai − Bi)²)

In 2D: √((Ax−Bx)² + (Ay−By)²)
 
  • Result range: 0 to ∞
  • 0 = identical vectors (same point)
  • Larger value = further apart
  • Sensitive to magnitude — long vectors score higher distances
  • Better when vectors are normalised to unit length

Section 4 of 6

Guided experiments

Each experiment isolates one behaviour. Click "Load settings" to pre-set the sliders, then observe what changes and what stays the same. Read the prediction before loading — then verify it.

1 The magnitude test — cosine stays fixed, Euclidean grows
Setup: Set both vectors to the exact same direction angle (e.g. 45°). Now change only the magnitudes — make one very long and one very short.

Prediction: Cosine similarity stays at 1.000 because the angle between the vectors is zero. Euclidean distance grows larger as the tips move further apart.

What this teaches: Cosine similarity does not care how long a sentence is. A one-word query and a full paragraph describing the same concept will score 1.0 if their directions agree. This is exactly why cosine is preferred for text search.
2 The direction test — both metrics change
Setup: Keep both magnitudes identical. Now rotate one vector away from the other — increase the angle gap to 45°, then to 80°.

Prediction: Both cosine similarity and Euclidean distance change together. As angle increases, cosine similarity falls toward 0 and Euclidean distance grows.

What this teaches: When magnitudes are equal, both metrics agree on the trend. The difference only appears when magnitudes differ. This is why Euclidean distance is acceptable when vectors have been normalised to unit length (magnitude = 1 for all vectors).
3 Related queries — high cosine, meaningful distance
Setup: Set vectors to represent "My package is missing" and "Where is my order?" — similar direction, slightly different magnitudes.

Prediction: Cosine similarity is high (above 0.90). Euclidean distance is moderate. ShopEasy's retrieval system would match these two queries to the same support tickets.

What this teaches: Two sentences describing the same real-world problem, using different words, land close together in embedding space. The semantic meaning is preserved in the direction of the vector, not the length.
4 Unrelated queries — low cosine, large distance
Setup: Rotate the two vectors to a large angle gap (around 75°) with different magnitudes, simulating "Where is my order?" versus "I want to cancel my gym membership".

Prediction: Cosine similarity drops well below 0.30. Euclidean distance is very large. Both metrics agree these are unrelated.

What this teaches: When queries describe entirely different problems, their embeddings point in very different directions. Both metrics signal low similarity, but cosine does so reliably regardless of how long either sentence is.

Section 6 of 6

Key differences, common misconceptions, and when to use each

The last thing to settle before you leave: knowing which metric to reach for, and which wrong assumptions to avoid.

Cosine similarity

Normalises away magnitude. Two vectors of length 1 and length 1000 can have cosine similarity = 1.0 if they point the same way. This is the feature, not a bug.

A score of 0.85 does not mean "85% similar" in any absolute sense. It means the angle between the two vectors has a cosine of 0.85, which corresponds to an angle of about 32°.

Rule of thumb: above 0.90 = highly similar; 0.70–0.90 = related; below 0.50 = weakly related; below 0.30 = unrelated.

Euclidean distance

Sensitive to magnitude. A long sentence and a short sentence describing the same concept can have a high Euclidean distance even if their meaning is identical.

However, when all vectors are normalised to unit length (a common pre-processing step), Euclidean distance becomes mathematically equivalent to cosine distance up to a constant factor.

Safe to use when: all vectors are unit-normalised, or you are working with low-dimensional feature vectors where magnitude genuinely carries meaning.
When to use which metric

Use cosine similarity when

  • Comparing text embeddings from any language model
  • Vectors have different magnitudes (different sentence lengths, document sizes)
  • Semantic direction matters more than absolute vector size
  • Building a RAG retrieval system (most common case)
  • Using Pinecone, Weaviate, or FAISS for similarity search
  • Working in high-dimensional embedding spaces (300–1536 dims)

Use Euclidean distance when

  • All vectors have been unit-normalised before comparison
  • Working with low-dimensional tabular feature vectors
  • Clustering algorithms such as k-means (which default to Euclidean)
  • Image embeddings from CNNs (often pre-normalised)
  • Physical measurements where distance genuinely means "far apart"
  • When the vector database or library you use defaults to it
Common misconceptions — corrected
Myth "Higher cosine similarity always means a better match." Fact Cosine similarity is relative. What counts as "high" depends on the embedding model, the domain, and the task. Always calibrate against examples from your dataset.
Myth "Euclidean distance is wrong for text." Fact Euclidean distance works fine for unit-normalised embeddings. Many vector databases normalise vectors at write time, making Euclidean and cosine equivalent in practice.
Myth "Cosine similarity of 1.0 means the sentences are identical." Fact It means the vectors point in exactly the same direction, not that the sentences share the same words. Many different sentences can produce parallel embedding vectors if their meaning aligns.
Myth "Dot product is the same as cosine similarity." Fact Dot product equals the product of the two magnitudes times cosine similarity. It conflates direction and length. Cosine similarity divides out the magnitudes; dot product does not.

The one-paragraph summary

Cosine similarity and Euclidean distance are both valid answers to "how similar are two vectors?" but they ask slightly different questions. Cosine asks: do these vectors point in the same direction? Euclidean asks: how far apart are their tips? For text embeddings — where the length of a sentence has nothing to do with its meaning — cosine similarity is almost always the right choice, because it ignores magnitude entirely. Euclidean distance becomes equally valid once all vectors are normalised to unit length, at which point the two metrics become mathematically equivalent up to a constant.

In the ShopEasy retrieval system, cosine similarity is what allows "My package is missing" and "Where is my order?" to retrieve the same support tickets, even though the embedding vectors have different lengths. The customer's intent — not the exact words used — is encoded in the direction of the vector, and cosine similarity is the tool designed to measure exactly that.
